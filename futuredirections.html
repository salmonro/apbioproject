Future Directions

Lingering Question +
Lingering question: How do we make the trees light up only at night and control its bioluminescence? MIT engineers (MIT 2017) are planning on inserting nanoparticles with luciferase inhibitors to “turn off” bioluminescence when desired
Circadian System Idea +
Can we utilize natural circadian rhythm systems in plants so that plants “glow” in the night and do not glow in the dark? Can luciferase be activated at certain times of the day and inactivated at others? -> light, temperature, and other conditions. “Molecular clock” proteins can activate or inactivate some of the fluorescent proteins like luciferase Specialized glands/organs that contain luciferase, oxygen, luciferin, hispidin and necessary components. Possibly in the leaves
Circadian Proteins +
ZEITLUPE (ZTL): Blue-light photoreceptor proteins that regulate the circadian clock and flowering. Synchronizes plant internal biological clocks with outside signals and day-night cycle CCA1, LHY, TOC1. CCA1 and LHY are transcription factors for morning genes. TOC1 is a transcription repressor that peaks in the evening (https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1061320) The DET1 protein in Arabidopsis may also play a role in regulating plant circadian systems by repressing “evening” genes such as CCA1/LHY. Basically, DET1 binds to transcriptional regulators including COP10 and DDB1 to form a transcriptional co-repressor complex and repress expression of transcription inhibitors (like TOC1) which then subsequently suppresses expression of CCA1. Turns evening genes off in the morning (https://www.cell.com/molecular-cell/fulltext/S1097-2765(11)00536-3?_returnURL=https%3A%2F%2Flinkinghub.elsevier.com%2Fretrieve%2Fpii%2FS1097276511005363%3Fshowall%3Dtrue)
Questions Section +
Question: what makes an evening gene an “evening” gene and what makes a “morning” gene a morning gene?
Ions +
Can we use ions to control these fluorescent genes? “Nutrient ions, such as Ca2+, Mg2+, and Fe2+, are also found to act in regulatory relationships between metabolic signals and the circadian clock” (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10181312/) Rhythmic Mg2+ fluctuations control carbon fixation and sugar accumulation in rice chloroplasts (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10181312/) Mg2+ is also an essential cofactor for ATP-dependent enzymes involved in fluorescence in firefly enzymes like luciferase. Binds ATP and facilitates the adenylation of luciferin (https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/firefly-luciferase)
Temperature and Circadian Rhythm +
Several circadian clock genes, including CCA1, LHY, GI, PRR7, PRR9, have been shown to interact with temperature signals INTERESTING CONNECTION TO GFP: “For example, ELF3, a member of the EC, functions as a thermosensor. At higher temperatures, ELF3 fused with green fluorescent protein forms speckles within a few minutes in response to higher temperatures” (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10181312/) Some genes like CCA1 and LHY may regulate circadian systems through rhythmic histone modification of various clock genes like SDG2 and JMJ14 (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13059-019-1777-1)
